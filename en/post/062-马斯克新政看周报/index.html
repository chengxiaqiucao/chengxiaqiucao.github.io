Musk's Weekly Report Mandate and What Weekly Reports Really Are

Musk's new policy requires every federal employee to send a weekly report listing five things done last week. Why does this move feel so familiar to wage earners? In this post, let's talk about weekly reports.

Feb 28, 2025
5 minute read
中文

Introduction

One of the hottest topics online these past couple of days has to be Musk’s new DOGE policy — the now-famous “what did you do last week” email requiring every federal employee to send a weekly report listing the five things they did last week, cc’ing their supervisor! Miss it twice in a row and it’s treated as a resignation!

And by now more than 1 million U.S. civil servants have already sent this weekly report! 😂😂😂

As corporate wage slaves, we know this routine all too well — but come to think of it, why do weekly reports even exist?

What Is the Essence of the Weekly (or Daily) Report?

For these U.S. civil servants, writing a weekly report may still be something of a novelty, but for a business-born boss like Musk, weekly reports are a long-standing favorite — he rolled out similar policies at Tesla and Twitter years ago.

So why, from a company’s or a boss’s point of view, do they want to see employees’ weekly reports?

Take a software testing department as an example; the value of a weekly report mainly shows up in the following areas:

  1. Risk control

This should be the part every Leader cares about most in a weekly report. Through weekly reports, you can know the overall progress of the whole project and get a grip on overall quality risks — such as testing delays or insufficient quality exposure. The weekly-report level, unlike a daily report, isn’t overly detailed, so it’s a good input for keeping a handle on the overall status.

  1. Resource optimization

By aggregating and analyzing weekly-report data, a Leader can also grasp how much effort every member is putting in, making it easier to dynamically allocate testing resources.

And this is also the focus of all the attention on Musk’s new policy, because the result the weekly reports point to is that underworked federal employees may get fired 🤪🤪

  1. Decision basis

A weekly report usually also contains some quantitative metrics, such as testing progress, defect data, coverage, escape rate, external support, and other quality data that can be captured or actively reported. These data quantify the current quality state and present it visually, serving as important support for decision-making.

I. Weekly Work Summary

  Test execution

    - Completed iterative testing of the heart-rate monitoring module V2.3, covering 10 exercise scenarios, and found 2 critical defects (data delay exceeding the threshold, monitoring failure at low battery).
    - Ran compatibility testing on iOS/Android APP sync; 3 device models (including older ones) showed data loss issues.
    - Regression test pass rate 95%, with the remaining 5% being low-priority UI display issues.

  Defect management

    - Added 15 defects (2 urgent, 5 high priority), closed 8 defects; 3 of the remaining defects require joint fixes with the hardware team.

  Special testing

    - Completed stress testing of the OTA upgrade process; 1 firmware verification failure occurred after simulating 5 consecutive upgrades.
    - In power-consumption testing, the standby power of the sleep-monitoring function exceeded the standard by 20%.

II. Core Issues and Risks

  Blocking issues
    The heart-rate monitoring data delay defect creates a risk of version release delay and needs to be fixed and verified by the development team within 48 hours.

  Resource dependencies
    The hardware team's slow response to sensor issues may affect next week's power-consumption test plan.

III. Next Week's Plan

  - Execute V2.4 feature testing (blood-oxygen monitoring, emergency call).
  - Retest the remaining defects and drive joint debugging with the hardware team.
  - Launch user-scenario stress testing (simulating 1,000 devices uploading data concurrently).

The weekly report above is summarized from two angles — the project and the testing department — and the value it reflects can be summed up in the table below:

ElementValue to the ProjectValue to the Testing Department
Test execution progressClarifies version quality status and helps the PM assess whether release criteria are met.Quantifies team output, making resource allocation and efficiency optimization easier (e.g., adjusting test case priorities).
Defect distribution and trendsExposes module risks and pushes development to focus on core issues (e.g., a high defect rate in the heart-rate module calls for refactoring).Analyzes root causes of defects (code/design/environment) and drives test case library additions and process improvements (e.g., adding low-battery scenarios).
Risks and dependenciesWarns of delay risks and promotes cross-department collaboration (e.g., involving the hardware team).Wins support from upper management (e.g., requesting a longer test cycle or more device resources).
Special test resultsValidates non-functional requirements (e.g., power consumption, stability), avoiding hard hits to user experience.Accumulates performance baseline data to provide a basis for comparison in later versions (e.g., a power-consumption metric library).
Next week’s plan and goalsAligns with project milestones, keeping testing in sync with development (e.g., the blood-oxygen feature test plan matches the iteration schedule).Clarifies team division of labor, avoiding overlapping or missed tasks.

Summary

So, as an employee, there’s really nothing to resist about weekly reports — it’s just a perfectly normal management practice. For testers, the weekly report is both a “dashboard” for testing work (reflecting quality status in real time) and a “bridge” for cross-department collaboration (driving problem resolution and resource coordination), while also providing data support for optimizing long-term testing strategy.

When it all comes down to it, the goal is still status synchronization

Just like agile stand-ups, and the daily reports that traditional projects may also have, their purpose is actually the same; I’ve covered this in detail in earlier posts, so feel free to refer to those.

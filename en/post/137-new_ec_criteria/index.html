The New National Standard for E-Bikes - A Quality Perspective

Background

On December 1 this year, the newest national safety technical standard for e-bikes took effect and older-standard e-bikes were banned from sale. Yet the use of new-standard e-bikes has drawn widespread complaints from both riders and dealers.

The main revisions to the standard can be found in its foreword. It is clear that the new standard starts from road safety and adds a series of mandatory requirements covering speed, fire prevention, communications and passenger safety.

The Main Gripes About the New Standard

Hard to Carry a Passenger

The new standard imposes strict limits on vehicle dimensions and seat design, so many new models cannot carry a child. Families with two children in particular find it hard to seat a child in front of and behind the adult as they could on older e-bikes. For families who rely on an e-bike to take kids to and from school, the impact is significant.

Not Enough Storage Space

To meet the weight limits of the new standard, many new-standard e-bikes have to drop the rear rack space, leaving nowhere to put a raincoat, helmet or shopping bag. Practicality drops sharply.

The “Sheet-Metal Seat” Problem

Under the new standard, some models design the seat as removable flame-retardant foam in order to meet fire-retardancy and weight requirements, and it can easily be taken off at will.

Slow Speed, Poor Riding Experience

The new standard caps e-bikes at 25 km/h, and power output is cut once that speed is reached. Many riders complain that the riding experience is worse than a bicycle, and that on slopes or when quick acceleration is needed the power does not come through, which actually affects safety too. It is especially unfriendly to delivery riders and couriers.

Beyond the gripes above, there are also issues such as higher prices due to the new safety materials and the rather pointless mandatory pedals. We will not list them one by one.

A Quality Perspective

Looking at the complaints about the new standard, it is clear they are not baseless. Many of them do reveal genuinely unreasonable parts of the standard. From a market point of view, you could say the products launched under this standard are actually failed products.

But can we say the quality of such products is bad, or that tightening control from a safety angle is wrong?

As testers, most of us have run into the situation where a product has clearly not been adequately tested, or still has quite a few unresolved bugs, yet it is rushed out the door anyway.

Usually the testing role can only sigh that “the arm cannot twist the thigh”, and grumble that “the project does not care about quality”. But does the product team really not care about product quality?

What Is Quality?

So let us return to quality. What exactly is quality?

The definition in ISO 9000: Quality is the degree to which a set of inherent characteristics (the attributes a thing itself possesses) fulfils requirements.

So where is the key here? It is precisely the requirements in fulfils requirements. We usually understand those requirements as the requirements document, the needs explicitly stated in user stories, or some generally accepted standards and specifications.

But for a product, measuring quality is hard to assess as fulfilled or not fulfilled, because when we speak of quality we usually only say good or bad. It is not quantitative or qualitative; it is more of an experience.

So my view is that quality is a subjective experience in service of product value.

In other words, quality is an important component of product value, but it does not determine product value.

From that angle, products released with bugs suddenly make sense. The judgment of the product team is: although the product still has bugs and some quality problems, the value of shipping it outweighs the impact of those quality problems, so we accept this portion of quality problems in the current product.

Passed on to the user side, it is quite similar:

  • Mixue Bingcheng uses overnight lemons, but you cannot argue with its friendly prices!
  • This Burberry bag is as ugly as a woven sack, but the brand is high-end!
  • This software system is so unpleasant to use that I do not want to open it, but only this system lets me get my work done!

Back to the new-standard e-bikes above: quality and safety were considered thoroughly enough and the requirements are very detailed, but the core value users care about most was ignored! At that point, quality and safety become a flashy skill with no dragon to slay.

What Should We Do at Work?

So how do we do our work better when we are testing or doing QA?

It comes down to one sentence: evaluate quality in terms of user value.

The key is in terms of user value rather than in terms of the requirements document. Requirements are the main carrier of user value, but not a sufficient one.

And user value has both a single-user perspective and an overall perspective. Under the single-user and the overall perspective, quality requirements can conflict, and then trade-offs and balance are needed.

For example, a feature added for a small set of specific users may affect the experience of most other users. Then whether to add that feature involves a trade-off.

In cases like this, we should shift from constantly judging “right or wrong” in testing to constantly weighing “good or bad”.

No longer obsessing over whether the requirement is implemented correctly, but caring more about whether the code implementation embodies the user value that the requirement represents.

Closing Thoughts

The thoughts above are one person’s opinion, and they do not include detailed measures for implementation. The only point is that, looking at it now, quality is like enlightenment: the way that can be told is not the constant way. It was never easy after all.
